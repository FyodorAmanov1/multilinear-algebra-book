The Mock Turtle was sitting beside an enormous table. On one side lay three spoons labelled $u_1$, $u_2$, and $u_3$. On the other side lay four cups labelled $v_1$, $v_2$, $v_3$, and $v_4$.

“How many pairings can you make?” he asked.

“Twelve,” said Alice, counting carefully.

“Exactly,” said the Mock Turtle. “A spoon by itself is not a pairing. A cup by itself is not a pairing. But every spoon-cup combination gets its own place at the table.”

::: {.intuition}
The tensor product $U\otimes V$ is a new vector space generated by symbols $\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf v$, with one vector from each space and with relations that make the symbol linear in each factor. It is different from the space of ordinary ordered pairs. It does not choose one pairing. It builds a room for every possible pairing.
:::

## From two rules to one

Alice labelled $U,V,W$ as finite-dimensional real vector spaces. All coefficients here belong to $\mathbb R$. A bilinear map may return a vector in $W$, unlike a bilinear form, which returns a scalar. For example, the dot product is a bilinear map with $W=\mathbb R$.

The new product obeys $(\mathbf u+\mathbf u')\otimes\mathbf v=\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf v+\mathbf u'\otimes\mathbf v$ and $(c\mathbf u)\otimes\mathbf v=c(\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf v)=\mathbf u\otimes(c\mathbf v)$. The same distributive rule holds in the second factor. These relations make the conversion below possible.

So far Alice had met rules that ate two vectors:

$$
B:U\times V\to W.
$$

Those rules were bilinear: fair in each input separately.

The tensor product turns that two-input problem into a one-input problem. It creates a new space $U\otimes V$ and a linear map

$$
\widetilde{B}:U\otimes V\to W
$$

such that

$$
\widetilde{B}(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v})
=
B(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).
$$

::: {.intuition}
A tensor product is the room where a two-argument story can be told as a one-argument story.
:::

## The table of basic pairings

If $\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_m\}$ is a basis of $U$ and $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\}$ is a basis of $V$, then a basis of $U\otimes V$ is given by the symbols

$$
\mathbf{u}_i\otimes\mathbf{v}_j,\qquad 1\le i\le m,\quad 1\le j\le n.
$$

There are $mn$ of them.

So

$$
\dim(U\otimes V)=\dim U\cdot\dim V.
$$

![A tensor product builds a table of every possible pairing.](assets/figures/ch09-tensor-products.svg){width=100%}

::: {.blackboard}
If $\dim U=m$ and $\dim V=n$, then

$$
\dim(U\otimes V)=mn.
$$

Tensor products multiply dimensions rather than adding them.
:::

## What lives in the new room?

The Mock Turtle assigned a real weight $c_{ij}\in\mathbb R$ to every table place. Both sums below are finite: $i=1,\ldots,m$ and $j=1,\ldots,n$. For this tensor, the weights are its coordinates in the product basis.

A general element of $U\otimes V$ looks like

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\,\mathbf u_i\otimes\mathbf v_j.
$$

It is important to remember that not every such element can be written as a single product $\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}$. Elements that can are called **simple tensors** or **pure tensors**.

::: {.intuition}
A pure tensor is like a single note. A general tensor can be a chord: several notes sounding together.
:::

## How a product spreads

Alice used $\mathbf u=(1,2)$ and $\mathbf v=(3,4)$. The four coefficients were $3,4,6,8$. To avoid mixing the two spaces, call their standard bases $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2$ for $U$ and $\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2$ for $V$. Then
$$
\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf v
=3\,\mathbf e_1\otimes\mathbf f_1+4\,\mathbf e_1\otimes\mathbf f_2
+6\,\mathbf e_2\otimes\mathbf f_1+8\,\mathbf e_2\otimes\mathbf f_2.
$$
Every coefficient is a product of one coordinate from each input. In the general expansion below, $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R$.

If

$$
\mathbf{u}=a\mathbf{u}_1+b\mathbf{u}_2,
\qquad
\mathbf{v}=c\mathbf{v}_1+d\mathbf{v}_2,
$$

then

$$
\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}
=
ac\,\mathbf{u}_1\otimes\mathbf{v}_1
+ad\,\mathbf{u}_1\otimes\mathbf{v}_2
+bc\,\mathbf{u}_2\otimes\mathbf{v}_1
+bd\,\mathbf{u}_2\otimes\mathbf{v}_2.
$$

The product spreads into every possible combination of basis directions.

::: {.intuition}
The tensor product is a machine for making every possible pairing of directions, not merely one pairing.
:::

![The product (1,2) tensor (3,4) has coefficient table [[3,4],[6,8]].](assets/figures/ch09-expansion.svg){width=100%}

## The universal property

“Can every fair two-input rule use this room?” Alice asked. “Yes, and in exactly one way,” said the Turtle. For the dot product, the corresponding linear map sends $\mathbf e_i\otimes\mathbf f_j$ to $1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise. Applying it to Alice's four-term expansion gives $3+8=11$.

The tensor product is characterized by a simple promise:

For every bilinear map

$$
B:U\times V\to W,
$$

there is a unique linear map

$$
\widetilde{B}:U\otimes V\to W
$$

such that

$$
\widetilde{B}(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v})
=
B(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).
$$

::: {.intuition}
The universal property says: “Bring me your bilinear rule, and I will give you a linear rule in a larger room.”
:::

![The direct bilinear route and the route through the tensor product give the same result.](assets/figures/ch09-universal.svg){width=100%}

## Tensor rank

A nonzero pure tensor has tensor rank 1. The zero tensor has rank 0. A sum of pure tensors may have higher rank.

The familiar matrix rank is a special case. A matrix can be viewed as a tensor in

$$
\mathbb{R}^m\otimes\mathbb{R}^n,
$$

and its rank is the smallest number of pure tensors needed to write it as a sum.

Alice's table $\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\6&8\end{pmatrix}$ is an outer product and has rank one. But $\mathbf e_1\otimes\mathbf f_1+\mathbf e_2\otimes\mathbf f_2$ has coefficient matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. A single outer product has dependent columns, whereas these columns are independent, so this tensor needs two terms. This identification is made in the chosen bases; it does not make every two-factor tensor a linear operator without identifying one factor with a dual space.

::: {.blackboard}
If a tensor can be written as

$$
\sum_{r=1}^R \mathbf{u}_r\otimes\mathbf{v}_r,
$$

with $R$ as small as possible, then its rank is $R$.
:::

![The coefficient tables distinguish a pure rank-one tensor from a rank-two sum.](assets/figures/ch09-rank.svg){width=100%}

### Keep the scalars consistent

The same construction works for complex vector spaces, but both factors must use the same field. With $U,V$ over $\mathbb C$, the coefficients $c_{ij}$ belong to $\mathbb C$ and the product is taken over $\mathbb C$. Tensor rank counts the fewest pure summands over the chosen field; it is different from tensor **order**, which counts factors.

## Alice’s combination-table notes

1. A tensor product builds one new space from two old spaces.
2. Its basis consists of all ordered pairings.
3. Its dimension is the product of the dimensions.
4. It converts bilinear rules into linear ones.

“A table is useful,” said the Mock Turtle, “but people will keep changing the labels on the chairs.”
